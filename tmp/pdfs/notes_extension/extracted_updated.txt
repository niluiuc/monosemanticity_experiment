Monosemanticity and Robustness
Reading Notes and Project 1 Derivations
October 2026
Abstract
These notes examine Beyond Interpretability: The Gains of Feature Monosemanticity
on Model Robustness by Qi Zhang, Yifei Wang, Jingyi Cui, Xiang Pan, Qi Lei, Stefanie
Jegelka and Yisen Wang. They collect the relevant concepts, training objectives, experimental
protocols, toy-model derivations and numerical checks, and distinguish the reported results
from possible extensions to robustness and recursive training. Both arXiv:2410.21331v1
and the ICLR 2025 proceedings version are considered. The continuation develops feature-
detection risk, a resource-controlled superposition phase diagram, and a general interference
bound, with detailed proofs and executable Python code.
Scope
These notes assume familiarity with undergraduate linear algebra, derivatives and probability,
but do not assume prior mechanistic-interpretability or contrastive-learning experience. The
aim is to derive the toy analysis, distinguish the claims from the evidence, and identify possible
research extensions.
The central scientific question is general: does encouraging features to represent more
coherent concepts also improve a model’s behavior when training data or test inputs
are imperfect? Most of the paper is about image representation learning and robustness. An
additional language-model experiment studies safety after fine-tuning. Safety is therefore one
application in this paper, not the definition of the entire research direction.
Three distinctions are used throughout. A paper result is reported by the authors. An
independent derivation is worked out here from stated assumptions. An open research claim
is not established by either. In particular, a plausible geometric story is not automatically a
theorem about a trained diffusion model.
These notes cover both versions of the paper, including their appendices. The original arXiv
version has 25 pages; the proceedings version has 33. Experimental values quoted as “v1” come
from the linked arXiv paper. The conference version changes some tables and adds analyses.
This distinction prevents an apparent discrepancy from being mistaken for a replication failure.
The numerical demonstrations in these notes are small CPU simulations and exact calculations.
They do not reproduce the paper’s ResNet or Llama training runs.

1
Research question and motivation
Focus: Explain the motivation without confusing interpretability, sparsity, robustness and safety.
Paper reference: Both versions, abstract and Sections 1–2.
1.1
Start with a representation
A neural network maps an input, such as an image, to a vector. Write this as
r = fθ(u) ∈Rd.
(1.1)
Here u is the observed input, θ is the collection of learned parameters and r is an intermediate
representation. A downstream classifier uses r to predict a class. The coordinates of r are often
called neurons, although a useful feature can be a direction across many coordinates.
A concept is a property of the input that we care about: a wheel, fur texture, a syntactic
pattern, or a task-relevant latent factor. A feature is an operational variable or direction that
responds to such a property. These are not synonyms by definition. Calling a direction a “cat
feature” requires evidence about what activates it and what changing it does.
An interpretable representation makes these relationships easier to identify. A robust represen-
tation supports reliable prediction despite specified changes. The paper asks whether these two
benefits can occur together, challenging the intuition that interpretability necessarily sacrifices
predictive usefulness.
1.2
Four kinds of robustness
1. Noisy training labels. The examples may be ordinary images, but their labels are
sometimes wrong. The question is how reliably a classifier can be learned from these data.
2. Corrupted inputs. Noise changes the input image. This is different from changing the
label and different from perturbing an internal feature vector.
3. Distribution shift. Sketch or stylized images differ systematically from pretraining images.
A model may rely on texture when the shifted domain requires shape.
4. Limited supervision. Few labeled examples provide less information for estimating the
downstream decision rule.
Adversarial robustness is another well-defined category: an adversary chooses a perturbation to
maximize damage subject to a budget. The source paper’s central vision experiments are not a
proof of adversarial robustness. A noisy-label improvement does not establish resistance to a
worst-case ℓ2 attack.
In the language-model arm, harmful responses are scored after fine-tuning. This tests a
particular safety-related property under a particular prompt set and evaluator. It does not
convert all the paper’s image experiments into AI-safety experiments.
1.3
The empirical argument
The paper compares a conventional contrastive representation with representations modified to
encourage monosemanticity. One route imposes nonnegativity in contrastive learning. Another
learns sparse features with an autoencoder. It then asks whether downstream robustness improves.
The toy analysis offers a possible explanation: packing multiple predictive signals into one neuron
can be beneficial on clean data but can increase sensitivity to certain corruptions.
5

This is a combination of empirical evidence and a deliberately small mathematical example. It
is not a general theorem that any increase in monosemanticity improves any notion of robustness
in every network.
1.4
Connection to the proposed research
The first proposed project asks for a more explicit tradeoff: when does storing more concepts in
fewer dimensions help, and when does interference become too costly? The second asks what
happens to those features across repeated training on generated data. The paper motivates the
first question directly. It motivates the second indirectly through representation geometry; it
does not study recursive model collapse.
The existing paper supplies the motivation and initial evidence. The proposed extension
requires a broader quantitative mechanism and a tested connection to recursive training.
Discussion question. State one robustness question about ordinary noisy data and one about an
adversary. Name the random variable or optimization problem that differs.

Features, sparsity and superposition
Focus: Translate the geometric vocabulary into precise objects, and understand why nonlinearity
matters.
Paper reference: Section 2 and the toy setup; background: Elhage et al., Toy Models of Superposition.
2.1
A model where the concepts are known
Let x ∈Rn contain genuine, independently specified latent features. An encoder stores them in
m dimensions:
h = Wx,
W ∈Rm×n,
h ∈Rm.
(2.1)
The ith column wi is the direction used to store feature i. A tied decoder reconstructs
ˆx = ReLU(W ⊤h + b) = ReLU(Gx + b),
G = W ⊤W.
(2.2)
The name “tied” means that the decoder uses the transpose of the encoder. An arbitrary
autoencoder need not satisfy this constraint.
The Gram matrix G measures pairwise inner products. Its diagonal entries are squared column
lengths; its off-diagonal entries describe overlap. It is symmetric, positive semidefinite and has
rank at most m.

Monosemanticity: a neuron or feature responds to a coherent concept rather than many

more economical. Conversely, nonnegativity does not by itself prove that each neuron represents
one concept.

2.4
Why the ReLU is doing real work
Take two nonnegative features and a single latent coordinate, W = (1, −1). Then
h = x1 −x2,
ˆx = (max(h, 0), max(−h, 0)).
(2.6)
If only one feature is active, it is reconstructed exactly. If both are active, cancellation loses their
shared magnitude. The representation is excellent when simultaneous activations are uncommon.
A purely linear decoder cannot perform this conditional separation.
7

The pair is also a useful answer to the hot/cold question. If hot and cold are two nonnegative,
potentially co-occurring features, cancellation can lose information. If they are defined as the
signs of one temperature variable, a signed coordinate can be completely appropriate. The
feature definition and task decide whether the subtraction is an error.
2.5
A capacity constraint
If m < n, an exactly diagonal G can have at most m nonzero diagonal entries. A monosemantic
baseline at fixed dimension must therefore drop some features, unless the task or definition
changes. Comparing superposition with G = In while retaining m < n gives the monosemantic
system impossible capacity. This is one reason a tradeoff diagram must include lost concepts,
not only interference among retained ones.
Discussion question. Explain how two nonnegative sparse features can share one signed coordinate,
and identify precisely the input on which reconstruction fails.

3.3
The nonnegative modification
Nonnegative contrastive learning restricts the representation entering the contrastive objective
to nonnegative coordinates, typically through a ReLU. For two nonnegative vectors, the dot
product is a sum of nonnegative terms. A negative contribution cannot cancel a positive one in
that similarity.
This can encourage a representation with more distinct, additive features. It is an inductive
bias, not an algebraic equivalence between nonnegativity and monosemanticity. Dense positive
vectors can still respond to many concepts. ReLU can also introduce inactive units and change
optimization, scale and capacity.
The right empirical comparison therefore measures semantic behavior, clean accuracy and
robustness together. If nonnegative features improve robustness, the next scientific question is
which changed property is responsible: sparsity, sign constraints, semantic selectivity, optimization
or a combination.
3.4
Pretraining, linear probing and fine-tuning
Pretraining learns the representation. Linear probing freezes it and trains only a classifier.
Fine-tuning updates the representation as well. These operations answer different questions.
The cross-entropy objective for a labeled example (r, y) is
LCE = −log
exp(a⊤

P

The minus sign is essential when minimizing. Some displayed cross-entropy expressions in the
paper omit it. The intended learning operation is conventional negative log likelihood; the
omission should not be copied into an implementation.
Freezing the representation isolates its usefulness under noisy supervision more cleanly than up-
dating everything. Fine-tuning asks whether the benefit survives adaptation. Neither experiment
alone proves that semantic coherence is the unique causal mechanism.
3.5
A practical detail with scientific consequences
The conference appendix compares features before and after the projector. The location matters:
the monosemanticity-promoting transformation acts at a specific point. A downstream test on
an earlier representation can miss the intervention’s effects. In a reproduction, record the exact
tensor used by the probe, its normalization, and its dimensionality.
Discussion question. Draw the pipeline from augmentation to encoder, projector, nonnegative
transformation and classifier. Indicate which parameters move during a linear probe.

Focus: Understand both alternative interventions and what they can and cannot identify.
Paper reference: Methods, language-model experiments and implementation appendices.
4.1
Sparse autoencoder mathematics
A sparse autoencoder takes a pretrained representation r and constructs sparse coefficients a:
a = TopK(Wer + be),
ˆr = Wda + bd.
(4.1)
9

TopK retains a prescribed number of entries according to the implementation’s activation
convention. In a nonnegative SAE, it is applied to nonnegative activations. The reconstruction
loss is
LSAE = E ∥r −ˆr∥2
2 .
(4.2)
Some formulations instead penalize an ℓ1 norm; the paper uses a TopK-style constraint. The
exact bias convention differs between the displayed versions. Do not substitute one formulation
for another silently when reproducing results.
The columns of Wd are dictionary directions. Their coefficients say which directions are active
for each input. A sparse dictionary can reveal features that are mixed in the original neuron basis.
But a successful reconstruction is not sufficient to establish faithful recovery of the network’s
causal computation.
4.2
Why reconstruction and interpretability compete
Reducing the number of active coefficients limits how much information can pass through the
autoencoder. It may encourage selectivity, but it can discard fine detail. Increasing dictionary
width adds representational capacity, but feature splitting or duplicated directions can make
semantic accounting difficult.
A useful evaluation reports at least reconstruction quality, activity/coverage, semantic selec-
tivity and downstream behavior. A feature that fires once on one correctly labeled image has
perfect apparent label purity but poor coverage. A dictionary can also make a representation
look cleaner by omitting information.
4.3
The paper’s operational semantic measure
The semantic-consistency idea asks whether samples activating a neuron tend to belong to a
common class. Schematically, if Aj is the set of samples assigned as activating neuron j, a purity
score is
cj = max

#{u ∈Aj : y(u) = c}
|Aj|

The paper specifies activation-selection details for its experiments. The mathematical point is
that this is a dataset- and label-dependent proxy. Class imbalance affects the baseline, and a
neuron can represent a meaningful shared property across several classes. Conversely, class-pure
activity can be based on a spurious background cue.
Semantic consistency therefore supports the interpretation that features became more class-
selective. It does not identify an intrinsic, universal monosemanticity scalar independent of the
dataset.
4.4
LoRA and the nonlinear variant
A conventional low-rank adaptation updates a frozen linear transformation by
y = W0x + ABx,
B ∈Rr×din,
A ∈Rdout×r.
(4.4)
Its parameter cost is r(din + dout). Because AB is a fixed matrix, it can ordinarily be merged
into W0 for inference.
The paper’s MonoLoRA inserts ReLUs into the adaptation branch:
y = W0x + ReLU(A ReLU(B ReLU(x))) .
(4.5)
10

The branch is now piecewise linear and input-dependent. It retains a low-rank parameterization
but is not a fixed low-rank matrix update globally. A single merged weight matrix cannot in
general reproduce all activation regions.
This intervention changes more than a verbal property called monosemanticity. It constrains
signs and activation patterns and modifies the function class. The evidence that it preserves
safety better under some fine-tuning conditions is empirical. A theoretical attribution solely to
semantic purity would require further controls.
4.5
Worked exercise
For scalar x, choose A = 1, B = −1 and W0 = 0. Ordinary LoRA gives −x. The MonoLoRA
branch above is zero for all x, since the nonnegative inner activation becomes nonpositive after
multiplication by B and is removed. This simple example demonstrates that inserting ReLUs is
a substantive architectural intervention.
Discussion question. Explain why an SAE dictionary’s Gram matrix is not automatically the
dynamical operator of the original network, and why MonoLoRA is generally not mergeable.

Reading the vision experiments carefully
Focus: Know what was trained, what was frozen, what was corrupted and what each plot demon-
strates.
Paper reference: v1 Section 3 and Appendix A; conference experimental additions are separated in Section
10.
5.1
The experimental architecture
The vision experiments use ResNet-18 representations on CIFAR-100 and ImageNet-100. The
reported pretraining setup uses 200 epochs, batch size 256, weight decay 10−4 and a two-layer
projector with hidden width 16,384 and output width 2,048. Linear evaluation runs for 50 epochs;
fine-tuning experiments run for 100 epochs. The SAE configuration uses 2,048-dimensional layers
and 256 active features in the main setup.
These choices establish a particular evidence base. They do not imply that the method was
tested on a large language model’s residual features with an SAE, or on a diffusion model. The
LLM arm uses MonoLoRA instead.

5.4
Out-of-distribution images
The v1 reported accuracies on sketch images are 23.6, 24.5 and 25.2 for CL, SAE and NCL; on
stylized images, 16.5, 17.2 and 18.5. These shifts are structured changes, so a gain suggests
more useful transferable information. It does not demonstrate a guarantee over every unseen
distribution.
A useful question is whether the gain comes from reduced reliance on texture, a different
effective dimension, or a more general effect of the representation. The paper’s measurements
motivate the mechanism but do not uniquely distinguish all alternatives.
5.5
Few-shot and fine-tuning evidence
Few-shot experiments restrict the labeled data available to the downstream learner. Fine-tuning
allows more of the model to adapt. The pattern of gains is consistent with the idea that cleaner
features reduce the burden on limited or imperfect supervision. But each setting changes the
estimation problem, so the correct unit of comparison is a matched protocol, not a single accuracy
aggregated across incompatible tasks.
5.6
How to read uncertainty
An accuracy difference of a few tenths of a point should be interpreted with run variation. The
conference version adds repeated-run evidence in several settings and changes some numerical
entries. Do not combine the mean from one version with an error bar from the other.
For the proposed study, independent training seeds are the units for comparing training inter-
ventions. Thousands of images from one trained model do not create thousands of independent
12

model-training replications. Bootstrap uncertainty over test examples is useful, but answers a
different question from variability across training seeds.
5.7
The feature-salience analysis
The paper also inspects the classifiers trained with 90% noisy labels. For each class it selects the
representation dimension with the largest classifier weight and displays the images that activate it
most strongly. The high-accuracy example is more class-coherent than the low-accuracy example.
A second analysis selects the most active dimension for each example and compares its semantic
consistency between correctly and incorrectly classified samples.
These analyses connect the observed prediction outcome to interpretable feature behavior.
They are correlational: class difficulty, frequency and the features selected by the classifier can
affect both purity and accuracy. Their appropriate role is mechanistic evidence that motivates a
controlled intervention, rather than a complete causal proof.
5.8
The larger toy experiment before the theorem
The paper’s empirical toy uses n = 40, m = 20 and inactive probability S = 0.2. Each nonzero
feature is uniform on [0, 1], and the classification label is the index of the largest feature. The
paper writes the evaluated linear representation as W ⊤Wx, which has n output coordinates
but rank at most m; the compressed code Wx itself has m coordinates. Keeping these distinct
prevents a dimension-counting confusion.
The non-superposed Gram matrix is diagonal with only m nonzero entries, so it stores only
half the concepts. The superposed representation stores more. A linear classifier is trained on
frozen features. The label-noise condition corrupts training labels by choosing another class;
the input-noise condition trains on clean examples and corrupts validation inputs. The plotted
accuracies are approximately 0.50 versus 0.58 on clean data, 0.31 versus 0.26 with label noise,
and 0.19 versus 0.16 with input noise, for mono versus poly respectively.
This is an actual accuracy tradeoff in a larger toy experiment. It should be distinguished from
the two-feature theorem’s J score. The empirical observation motivates the theory but does not
make that score an exact surrogate for accuracy.
Discussion question. For each experiment family, identify the intervention, the corruption, the
outcome and the strongest claim the result supports.

Language-model safety and the limits of attribution
Focus: Read the safety results as a specific experiment, without redefining the whole paper as a
safety project.
Paper reference: v1 language-model results and implementation appendix; conference additional MMLU
evaluation.
6.1
What is being compared
The authors fine-tune Llama-2-7B-chat with LoRA and MonoLoRA on SST-2 and Dolly. The
evaluation combines task performance with harmfulness judgments. SST-2 is a sentiment task;
Dolly measures instruction-following adaptation through a text-generation score. Neither task is
itself a training objective for eliminating harmful content.
The reported SST-2 setup uses 20 epochs, batch size 16 and learning rate 10−4. The adapters
use rank 8, scaling parameter 4 and dropout 0.1. The Dolly setup uses one epoch, batch size 4,
13

5,000 training examples and 1,000 inference examples. Query and value projections receive the
adapters. These details limit the inference: another rank, dataset, layer selection or training
budget may behave differently.

6.3
What an automated safety judge measures
ShieldGemma-9B and BeaverTails-7B supply model-based judgments. A judge is a measurement
instrument with its own mistakes, thresholds and coverage. It can support a comparison on a
fixed evaluation set. It does not certify safety under arbitrary deployment conditions.
The scientifically useful question is whether the result repeats across seeds, evaluators, prompt
families and matched task quality. A method can appear safer because it is less willing to answer,
because it learned a different style, or because an evaluator reacts differently to its wording.
These are possibilities to test, not reasons to dismiss the reported pattern.
6.4
Causal interpretation
MonoLoRA changes signs, gates and optimization as well as the purported semantic properties of
the adapter. To isolate semantic coherence as the driver, one would ideally compare interventions
that alter it while controlling alternative explanations, or measure a mediation pattern and then
test it with causal perturbations.
Safety may be a later application of this research. The central question remains the relation
between feature organization and reliable behavior, extended to recursive training. The question
does not require a safety benchmark or an agent system.
Discussion question. Explain why “better than LoRA after fine-tuning” and “safer than the base
model” are different claims. Use the Dolly values to demonstrate the difference.

9.4
A useful reading of the paper’s theory
The theory demonstrates that polysemantic and monosemantic representations can respond
differently to corruption under a specified separability measure. It offers a tractable mechanism
that accompanies the vision evidence. It does not establish the full p, n/m, Ii boundary proposed
for the extension, nor a theorem for recursive self-training.
The appropriate extension is to choose an operational outcome, derive its risk under explicit
assumptions, and test the predicted tradeoff. Sections 13–18 develop this approach for a small
feature-detection model.

Worked problem A: an atom changes the prior
Question. Why is the class-0 probability not 1/2? Solution. Exchange symmetry makes the
strict events X1 > X2 and X2 > X1 equally probable. Their probabilities sum to 1 −S2, since
only the zero-zero tie has positive tie probability. Each strict event therefore has probability
(1 −S2)/2. Assigning the tie to class 0 adds S2, giving (1 + S2)/2.

Synthesis and research extensions
Focus: Summarize the contribution and connect it to the two proposed research questions.
12.1
The paper in one connected explanation
The paper investigates whether more semantically coherent representations can improve practical
robustness rather than merely making models easier to inspect. It encourages such representations
through nonnegative contrastive learning and sparse autoencoders, and measures downstream
behavior under noisy labels, corrupted images, distribution shifts and limited supervision. A
separate nonlinear adapter intervention tests safety retention during language-model fine-tuning.
The reported patterns support a useful connection between representation organization and
reliable behavior.
The toy model explains one possible tradeoff. A one-dimensional representation containing
both features can separate clean classes better than one that drops a feature. Under certain
corruptions, its conditional moments change in a way that worsens a chosen separability score
relative to the simpler representation. The mathematical statement is deliberately narrower
than a general robustness theorem. The conference additions strengthen the empirical case and
provide a latent-class explanation for why nonnegative representations can arise.
12.2
Extension I: the superposition-to-robustness phase diagram
The superposition-to-robustness phase diagram asks for a boundary or bound as feature
frequency, capacity load and importance vary.
To go beyond the source paper, define an
operational risk, include the information lost by a monosemantic baseline, and derive the tradeoff
under stated assumptions. Nonlinear decoding must be retained where it is responsible for sparse
superposition’s benefit.
The source paper is the conceptual and experimental anchor. It should be cited as motivating
evidence, not described as a special case of a new theorem until the assumptions and metrics
genuinely contain its setting.
24

12.3
Extension II: model collapse and monosemanticity
Model collapse as monosemanticity decay asks whether feature organization predicts or
contributes to degradation across recursive training. The phrase is a research hypothesis, not an
established result: some loops preserve a distribution, some lose rare combinations, some blur or
amplify features, and a representation can change without a meaningful loss of quality.
The extension should define a training-and-generation operator, measure feature-specific errors
and derive how these errors propagate. A fixed Gram-matrix covariance identity is a useful
elementary calculation, but it does not by itself describe a retrained generative model.
12.4
The combined paper
The shared object is feature interference geometry. Its immediate effect is decoding reliability;
its repeated effect, under an explicitly defined generation mechanism, can be distributional drift.
The feature-detection derivations in Sections 13–18 establish the immediate-risk side of this
program. A recursive-training operator and a validated diffusion connection require a separate
extension; they are not inferred from the fixed-geometry formulas.
The structure remains mathematics, controlled toy validation, real-model evidence.
The derivations are not symbolic-regression fits. The real-model work is scientifically necessary
for the intended empirical story, even though its exact outcome cannot be promised before
experiments.
12.5
Questions for discussion

applied?
2. Which experiment concerns noisy labels, which concerns noisy inputs, and which concerns
safety?
3. Why can sparse features share dimensions, and why does the decoder matter?
4. What does the paper’s J measure, and why is it not an adversarial radius or classification-

5. Which empirical observations motivate a broader phase diagram?

13.2.4
Three distinct roles for importance

activation occurs. A rare concept can have a large penalty when missed. In a fixed model,
changing Ii changes the reported weighted risk without changing G. In a trained model, changing
the weights in the training loss can also change the learned geometry. Neither operation proves
that the optimizer must allocate an isolated direction to that concept. Those effects must be
separated in the analysis and experiments.
13.3
Three noise locations
1. Feature-space corruption: b or a continuous feature vector is perturbed before encoding.
A feature-space adversary is an idealized mathematical model.
2. Code-space corruption: h = Wb + ξ with ξ ∼N(0, σ2Im). This makes each matched
readout’s variance depend on Gii.
3. Observed-input corruption: pixels or waveforms change before a nonlinear encoder.
The encoder’s Jacobian and nonlinear activation changes matter.
These yield different formulas. A proof for the first cannot be described as a guarantee for the
third without an additional argument.

14.4
Interpretation: perturbations, sign, frequency and scale
Can an attacker move a perfectly isolated feature? Yes. If G = I, then Mi = 1, but
changing xi changes its reading. An ability to move the score does not imply Mi < 1.
Does a negative overlap count? In this unrestricted worst-case ℓ2 setting, Gij enters
squared, because an adversary can choose the harmful sign. For ordinary nonnegative data and a
threshold decoder, the sign can be beneficial or harmful depending on co-occurrence. The square
loses that distinction.
Does rarity determine Mi? No. At fixed G, Equation (14.4) has no activation probability
in it. Rarity may affect what G learns, or statistical collision probabilities, but it cannot be
inserted into this fixed-geometry identity by multiplication.
Is Mi scale invariant? It is invariant under a common rescaling W 7→aW, since all entries
of G scale by a2. Rescaling only one column changes its overlaps relative to its diagonal and
generally changes Mi. The diagonal has not disappeared physically merely because it is hidden
in normalized overlaps.

two targets, not the probability of a single binary failure event.

Importance, correlations and learned geometry
Importance enters total risk as P

importance-weighted. To predict a boundary purely from p, n/m, γ, one must characterize the
learned geometry as a function of these controls. This remains a substantive theorem target;

final claim.
If features are correlated, use conditional distributions given bi and the nuisance covariance. In
general, a single centered threshold no longer gives symmetric margins. Under recursive training,
initially independent features can become dependent after one generation. Do not keep applying
the independent-Bernoulli bound without checking its conditions.
