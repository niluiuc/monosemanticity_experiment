What this volume delivers
This volume keeps the two agreed projects and their shared monosemanticity motivation. It does not replace
them with a benchmark, an agent system or a different application area. It supplies a mathematically explicit
foundation that can support their development.
The exact formulas delivered here include: operational perturbation radii; reconstruction risk; a finite-state
feature-detection risk; a small, nonlinear superposition phase boundary with feature frequency and importance;
a general interference-based error bound; exact recursive toy dynamics; a bound connecting one-generation
feature errors to cumulative distribution drift; a local derivative for a genuinely retrained model; and a Gaussian
denoising calculation clarifying the diffusion connection.
These are finished conditional calculations, not a claim that the proposed conference contribution is already
established. Some are standard facts presented with proofs; others are worked combinations specialized to this
program. Their novelty has not been certified. The general learned-geometry phase diagram and a validated
monosemanticity-based predictor in real diffusion models remain the research targets.
That distinction is essential to using these notes productively. A theorem can be correct and still be too
elementary for a new paper. A promising experiment can still contradict a preferred mechanism. The purpose
of the derivations is to make the remaining research time test precise claims, not to promise an outcome that
has not been observed.
Project 1 update: exact scope of the completed mathematics
The added clean-training and noise chapters derive the finite bias profiles, dense-case optima, a certified low-
frequency geometry, the global clean frequency transition, and the at-least-two noise-crossing result. They
preserve the distinction between reconstruction-selected representations and downstream binary detection.
All new mathematics remains in this volume rather than only in working Markdown files. The arbitrary-
load/importance target and real-model transfer are not complete; the original Project 2 material is preserved
as background, with no new recursive-training experiment introduced.
The scientific question in plain language
When a model shares representational directions between concepts, it can store more information in limited
space. That sharing can also create confusion. We want to understand the conditions under which the gain
in coverage outweighs the cost in errors, and whether the same errors accumulate when generated outputs
become future training data.
This is a general learning-and-representation question. Safety is a possible application, but no adversary is
needed for the main study. Recursive training supplies the connection to the team’s self-improvement interests;
a self-consuming loop does not count as improvement unless quality actually improves. Vision diffusion provides
a real generative setting in which to test the mechanism.
Read these chapters in sequence
Chapters 1–3 establish definitions and operational interpretations. Chapters 4–6 derive fixed-geometry Project
1 results and a general interference bound. Chapters 7–8 derive clean-selected geometry, the global frequency
transition and the certified nonmonotone noise comparison. Chapters 9–12 derive Project 2 and the mathemat-
ical connection. Chapters 13–15 explain diffusion, controlled validation and the original eight-week execution
outline. The appendices give a formula ledger, exercises, numerical results and primary references.
No team member is assigned ownership in this document. The work has a continuous theoretical and experi-
mental thread, with room for complementary interests in interpretation, robustness, evaluation, post-training
and generative modeling.

1.1
The commitments that remain fixed
Project 1 seeks a robust-performance tradeoff in sparsity, feature-to-dimension load and impor-
tance decay. Project 2 seeks a representation-level account of recursive-training degradation

1.3
Three noise locations
1. Feature-space corruption: b or a continuous feature vector is perturbed before encod-

3. Observed-input corruption: pixels or waveforms change before a nonlinear encoder.
The encoder’s Jacobian and nonlinear activation changes matter.
These yield different formulas. A proof for the first cannot be described as a guarantee for the
third without an additional argument.

2.4
Interpreting isolation, rarity and signed overlap
Can an attacker move a perfectly isolated feature? Yes. If G = I, then Mi = 1, but
changing xi changes its reading. An ability to move the score does not imply Mi < 1.
Does a negative overlap count? In this unrestricted worst-case ℓ2 setting, Gij enters squared,
because an adversary can choose the harmful sign. For ordinary nonnegative data and a thresh-
old decoder, the sign can be beneficial or harmful depending on co-occurrence. The square loses
that distinction.
Does rarity determine Mi? No. At fixed G, Equation (2.3) has no activation probability
in it. Rarity may affect what G learns, or statistical collision probabilities, but it cannot be
inserted into this fixed-geometry identity by multiplication.
Is Mi scale invariant? It is invariant under a common rescaling W 7→aW, since all entries
of G scale by a2. Rescaling only one column changes its overlaps relative to its diagonal and
generally changes Mi. The diagonal has not disappeared physically merely because it is hidden
in normalized overlaps.
2.5

For a code-space perturbation h 7→h + δh, the tied readout changes by w⊤

(Gei)⊤Nx(Gei). These differ from each other and from the feature-space worst-case swing.
For a nonlinear observed-input map h = f(u), a local first-order calculation uses ∇u[w⊤

To obtain a finite-radius guarantee, bound the remainder or a Jacobian norm over the entire per-

8.11.5
The declared numerical branch-and-bound calculation
A bounded local minimizer, the clean bias, and the two finite boundary values provide feasible
incumbent losses. Local minimization is used only to obtain an upper bound. The interval

lower bound from Equation (8.47).

intervals whose lower bounds exceed the best upper bound are discarded. Both excluded-tail
bounds remain in the global comparison.
The declared floating-point slack is 10−12: numerical lower bounds are reduced and feasible
upper values increased by that amount.
Stop when the numerical global upper-minus-lower
gap is at most 10−8 per feature, or after 100,000 interval expansions. An unresolved gap or
excluded-tail check is preserved as a failure. Every interval, midpoint loss and gradient, lower
bound, pruning action, remaining interval and boundary comparison is archived.
What the numerical bound does and does not mean.
The floating slack is not outward-
rounded interval arithmetic and does not make this an exact-rational noisy-optimum theorem.
It is a numerical global-gap diagnostic, accompanied by independent gradient and Gaussian-
quadrature checks. The sign of a model comparison must be separated from the sum of its
global gaps and integration discrepancies before being interpreted as evidence.

of the form PW (Mg) = 0 need not imply the necessary smooth stationarity condition PW g = 0,
where PW is the Euclidean tangent projection. Coordinatewise preconditioning and Euclidean
projection do not generally commute. Small learning rates can reduce movement without re-
solving that distinction. This is a reason for the direct-gradient diagnostic; it is not a proof that
preconditioning is the sole cause of every observed failure.

8.22.3
Global bias localization under calibration
Both models now receive free population bias minimization at each noise level, with their en-
coders fixed. The following argument ensures that a local bias expansion describes the global
infimum.
Positive large biases have uniformly coercive squared loss; far-left biases have loss approaching p,
which is separated from the relevant minima by a positive amount on the compact probability
interval. Thus global bias minimizers can be localized to a common compact set. ReLU is
Lipschitz, and coupling clean and noisy scores gives uniform convergence of their objectives
there as k, σ →0. The limiting strong-feature optimum is bias zero, uniquely; the weak-feature
optimum is bias p0, uniquely. Consequently global minimizers are in neighborhoods of these
biases.
In a small fixed neighborhood of the strong clean bias, target-one states have mean bounded
away from zero. Their squared-error losses have curvature 2p and their Gaussian gate errors
are exponentially small. Target-zero terms are squared ReLUs, whose derivative is globally two-
Lipschitz and whose noise-induced objective change is at most the score variance. Therefore
|fσ −f0| ≤Lσ2 plus exponentially small terms, uniformly in that neighborhood. The clean
objective is strongly convex there, with f0(β) −f0(β∗) ≥p(β −β∗)2. Comparing a global noisy
minimizer with the clean bias gives |βσ −β∗| = O(σ) = o(k).
The clean strong bias has a uniform positive distance proportional to k from both nearby target-
zero activation boundaries. Localization therefore places the global noisy minimizer in the same
active branch. In that branch the clean derivative is 2D(β −β∗), while the noise correction to it
is exponentially small because each gate is many Gaussian standard deviations away. The noisy
curvature stays bounded away from zero. The optimal bias displacement and its improvement
over the frozen clean bias are therefore exponentially small, not order σ2.
For the weak output, all conditional means remain bounded away from zero near its globally
localized bias p0. The linear-quadratic variance is independent of its bias; gate corrections and
bias changes are again exponentially small. The positive importance factor η does not change the
scalar minimizing bias and is bounded above and below uniformly. Thus calibration of sharing
leaves its leading noise coefficient D0 unchanged. These localization steps exclude a distant
competing bias minimum rather than assume local root finding establishes a global optimum.

13.6
Existing work the new claim must distinguish itself from
The MAD work studies self-consuming generative loops and the role of real-data supply. The
accumulating-data work shows that replacement and accumulation can behave differently. A
June 2026 paper by Khelifa, Turner and Venkataramanan already gives a spectral characteriza-
tion of recursive diffusion collapse driven by reverse-process truncation, even under ideal score
estimation. Therefore “the first spectral account of diffusion collapse” is not an available claim
based on this reading.
The intended distinction is a tested mechanism involving sparse semantic features, interference,
robust decoding and subsequent retention. Keep reverse truncation fixed and check whether it
explains the apparent effect before attributing that effect to feature geometry.
Before moving on. Identify the covariance term that posterior sampling restores
in the Gaussian example. Explain why posterior means alone lose it.

Stage D: one controlled image generator
Before a general image dataset, use images generated from known factors: shape identity, lo-

the known rendering factors or a validated held-out evaluator to measure marginal and joint
fidelity. Test feature robustness on fixed real reference images under controlled image corruptions
or denoising conditions. Avoid defining a concept detector solely from the same model whose
collapse is being measured.
14.5
Stage E: a small natural-image diffusion demonstration
Use one tractable vision setting, such as CIFAR-scale images, with a small established diffusion
implementation. A pilot should measure wall-clock cost for one generation before committing
to a long recursion. The primary comparison might use two representation/training conditions,
two real-data fractions and three seeds over a short, informative horizon. The exact number
of generations is determined by the pilot and the observed transition, not by a promise made
before measuring compute.
This remains a real-model validation arm. If it fails to show the predicted mechanism, report
that limitation and investigate the mismatch. It should not be reclassified as optional success
evidence only after seeing unfavorable results.
14.6
How to measure geometry consistently
Fix the layer, diffusion-time sampling distribution and reference inputs. Fit a common diagnostic
dictionary across checkpoints, or explicitly align independently learned dictionaries and report
alignment uncertainty. Otherwise feature permutations, rescalings, splitting and merging can
masquerade as semantic decay.
For a common dictionary, report reconstruction fidelity at every checkpoint: a fixed dictionary
can fail because the basis changes, even if useful computation is preserved. With checkpoint-
specific dictionaries, distinguish actual concept changes from changed coordinate systems. Use
direct labeled-factor behavior as a reference wherever possible.

Chapter 14. Experiments that test the derivations
14.7
Interventions that support a mechanism
An intervention should change the hypothesized interference while monitoring clean quality,
norms and coverage. Examples include a controlled coherence penalty in a toy model, a sparsity-
promoting module, or a targeted direction manipulation with a random-direction control. A
penalty that orthogonalizes everything may simply reduce stored information, so compare at
matched capacity and inspect feature loss.
The strongest evidence is a linked change: the intervention alters measured geometry, changes
the predicted one-step errors and changes later feature retention in the predicted direction. A
correlation between low purity and late generations alone is much weaker.
14.8
Evaluation and uncertainty
Report training seeds as independent replications. For rare events, provide sample counts and
uncertainty; zero observed events is not proof of zero probability. If a concept has true frequency
p, a sample of size N contains none with probability (1 −p)N, approximately e−Np. To make
this probability at most δ, one needs roughly N ≥log(1/δ)/p. This calculation belongs in the
sampling plan.
For generative quality, combine a distributional image metric with concept-frequency and joint-
frequency measures. Aggregate metrics can miss tail loss. Use held-out real reference data for
evaluation, and never select thresholds or order parameters on the final test outcomes without
reporting that selection.

Mixture covariance includes a mean term
If real data have mean µ0 and covariance C0, while generated data have mean µQ and covariance
CQ, a mixture with real fraction α has covariance
Cmix = αC0 + (1 −α)CQ + α(1 −α)(µ0 −µQ)(µ0 −µQ)⊤.
(B.3)
The last term is between-component variation. Omitting it can make a drifting mean look like
an unexplained covariance change. The finite-state kernel automatically accounts for this when
moments are computed from the full law.

Appendix B. Further derivations and worked exercises
B.5
Exercise 3: why a largest overlap and a sum differ
Compare one nuisance with overlap a and four nuisances with overlap a/2. Their total squared
overlap is equal. Under isotropic unrestricted feature noise, the readout variance can agree.
Under sparse nonnegative activations, their distributions of interference differ: one large jump
is not the same random variable as a sum of four smaller jumps. The Bernstein bound records
both Vi and Bi to reflect this distinction.
B.6
Exercise 4: solve the mixed fixed kernel
Starting from Pg+1 = αP0+(1−α)KPg, repeatedly substitute the previous step to obtain a finite
geometric series plus (1 −α)gKgP0. The final term vanishes for α > 0 in the probability-vector
norm, leaving the fixed-point series in Chapter 10. This is a statement about repeated use of a
fixed channel; refitting generally changes K and prevents that same closed form.
B.7
Exercise 5: covariance does not identify concepts
Take a two-dimensional isotropic Gaussian and rotate it by 45 degrees. The covariance and
distribution are unchanged, but coordinate axes are different.
A covariance-based criterion
alone cannot say which axes correspond to meaningful concepts. Non-Gaussian planted factors
or external semantic measurements are necessary for that question.
B.8
Exercise 6: a sharply stated falsification
Suppose an SAE overlap score predicts late-generation image degradation until reconstruction
error and feature frequency are included, after which it adds no held-out predictive information.
This does not invalidate the exact toy algebra. It weakens the claim that the chosen geometry
measure explains real-model collapse beyond simpler measurements. The paper should report
and analyze that distinction rather than call the transfer confirmed.
