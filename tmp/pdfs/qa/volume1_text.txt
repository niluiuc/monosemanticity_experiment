How to use this course
This course assumes comfort with undergraduate linear algebra, derivatives and probability, but does not
assume prior mechanistic-interpretability or contrastive-learning experience. The intended reader wants
to understand the paper well enough to derive its toy analysis, distinguish its claims from its evidence,
and build a research contribution from it.
The central scientific question is general: does encouraging features to represent more coherent
concepts also improve a model’s behavior when training data or test inputs are imperfect?
Most of the paper is about image representation learning and robustness. An additional language-model
experiment studies safety after fine-tuning. Safety is therefore one application in this paper, not the
definition of the entire research direction.
The course is organized as twelve lectures, suitable for six weeks at two lectures per week. That is a
teaching schedule, not a requirement to postpone research for six weeks. A fast first pass through Lectures
1–4 and 7–10 gives the foundation for Volume II; return to the remaining derivations and exercises while
experiments begin.

Chapter 1
What the paper is actually asking
Learning objective.
Explain the motivation without confusing interpretability,
sparsity, robustness and safety.
Source map: Both versions, abstract and Sections 1–2..
1.1
Start with a representation
A neural network maps an input, such as an image, to a vector. Write this as
r = fθ(u) ∈Rd.
(1.1)
Here u is the observed input, θ is the collection of learned parameters and r is an intermediate
representation. A downstream classifier uses r to predict a class. The coordinates of r are often
called neurons, although a useful feature can be a direction across many coordinates.
A concept is a property of the input that we care about: a wheel, fur texture, a syntactic pattern,
or a task-relevant latent factor. A feature is an operational variable or direction that responds
to such a property. These are not synonyms by definition. Calling a direction a “cat feature”
requires evidence about what activates it and what changing it does.
An interpretable representation makes these relationships easier to identify. A robust represen-
tation supports reliable prediction despite specified changes. The paper asks whether these two
benefits can occur together, challenging the intuition that interpretability necessarily sacrifices
predictive usefulness.
1.2
Four kinds of robustness
1. Noisy training labels.
The examples may be ordinary images, but their labels are
sometimes wrong. The question is how reliably a classifier can be learned from these data.
2. Corrupted inputs. Noise changes the input image. This is different from changing the
label and different from perturbing an internal feature vector.
3. Distribution shift. Sketch or stylized images differ systematically from pretraining im-
ages. A model may rely on texture when the shifted domain requires shape.
4. Limited supervision. Few labeled examples provide less information for estimating the
downstream decision rule.
Adversarial robustness is another well-defined category: an adversary chooses a perturbation to
maximize damage subject to a budget. The source paper’s central vision experiments are not
a proof of adversarial robustness. A noisy-label improvement does not establish resistance to a
worst-case ℓ2 attack.

Chapter 1. What the paper is actually asking
In the language-model arm, harmful responses are scored after fine-tuning. This tests a particular
safety-related property under a particular prompt set and evaluator. It does not convert all the
paper’s image experiments into AI-safety experiments.
1.3
The empirical argument
The paper compares a conventional contrastive representation with representations modified to
encourage monosemanticity. One route imposes nonnegativity in contrastive learning. Another
learns sparse features with an autoencoder. It then asks whether downstream robustness im-
proves. The toy analysis offers a possible explanation: packing multiple predictive signals into
one neuron can be beneficial on clean data but can increase sensitivity to certain corruptions.
This is a combination of empirical evidence and a deliberately small mathematical example. It is
not a general theorem that any increase in monosemanticity improves any notion of robustness
in every network.
1.4
Why this is the right starting point for your project
Your first project asks for a more explicit tradeoff: when does storing more concepts in fewer
dimensions help, and when does interference become too costly? Your second asks what happens
to those features across repeated training on generated data. The paper motivates the first
question directly. It motivates the second indirectly through representation geometry; it does
not study recursive model collapse.
This separation preserves the research direction. It tells us what the existing paper supplies and
what the new work must supply: a broader quantitative mechanism and a tested connection to
recursive training.
Before moving on. State one robustness question about ordinary noisy data and
one about an adversary. Name the random variable or optimization problem that
differs.

2.1
A model where the concepts are known
Let x ∈Rn contain genuine, independently specified latent features. An encoder stores them in
m dimensions:
h = Wx,
W ∈Rm×n,
h ∈Rm.
(2.1)
The ith column wi is the direction used to store feature i. A tied decoder reconstructs
ˆx = ReLU(W ⊤h + b) = ReLU(Gx + b),
G = W ⊤W.
(2.2)
The name “tied” means that the decoder uses the transpose of the encoder.
An arbitrary
autoencoder need not satisfy this constraint.
The Gram matrix G measures pairwise inner products. Its diagonal entries are squared column
lengths; its off-diagonal entries describe overlap. It is symmetric, positive semidefinite and has
rank at most m.
Figure 2.1: Original geometric illustration. Nonorthogonal directions can store more features
than there are dimensions. The interpretation of their interference depends on the decoder and
the data distribution.

2.4
Why the ReLU is doing real work
Take two nonnegative features and a single latent coordinate, W = (1, −1). Then

ˆx = (max(h, 0), max(−h, 0)).

A purely linear decoder cannot perform this conditional separation.

potentially co-occurring features, cancellation can lose information. If they are defined as the
signs of one temperature variable, a signed coordinate can be completely appropriate.
The
feature definition and task decide whether the subtraction is an error.

Chapter 2. Features, sparsity and superposition
2.5
A capacity constraint
If m < n, an exactly diagonal G can have at most m nonzero diagonal entries. A monosemantic
baseline at fixed dimension must therefore drop some features, unless the task or definition
changes. Comparing superposition with G = In while retaining m < n gives the monosemantic
system impossible capacity. This is one reason a tradeoff diagram must include lost concepts,
not only interference among retained ones.
Before moving on. Explain how two nonnegative sparse features can share one
signed coordinate, and identify precisely the input on which reconstruction fails.

Chapter 3. Contrastive learning and nonnegative features
3.3
The nonnegative modification
Nonnegative contrastive learning restricts the representation entering the contrastive objective
to nonnegative coordinates, typically through a ReLU. For two nonnegative vectors, the dot
product is a sum of nonnegative terms. A negative contribution cannot cancel a positive one in
that similarity.
This can encourage a representation with more distinct, additive features. It is an inductive
bias, not an algebraic equivalence between nonnegativity and monosemanticity. Dense positive
vectors can still respond to many concepts. ReLU can also introduce inactive units and change
optimization, scale and capacity.
The right empirical comparison therefore measures semantic behavior, clean accuracy and ro-
bustness together. If nonnegative features improve robustness, the next scientific question is
which changed property is responsible: sparsity, sign constraints, semantic selectivity, optimiza-
tion or a combination.
3.4
Pretraining, linear probing and fine-tuning
Pretraining learns the representation. Linear probing freezes it and trains only a classifier.
Fine-tuning updates the representation as well. These operations answer different questions.
The cross-entropy objective for a labeled example (r, y) is
LCE = −log
exp(a⊤
y r + by)
P
c exp(a⊤
c r + bc).
(3.4)
The minus sign is essential when minimizing. Some displayed cross-entropy expressions in the
paper omit it.
The intended learning operation is conventional negative log likelihood; the
omission should not be copied into an implementation.
Freezing the representation isolates its usefulness under noisy supervision more cleanly than
updating everything. Fine-tuning asks whether the benefit survives adaptation. Neither exper-
iment alone proves that semantic coherence is the unique causal mechanism.
3.5
A practical detail with scientific consequences
The conference appendix compares features before and after the projector. The location matters:
the monosemanticity-promoting transformation acts at a specific point. A downstream test on
an earlier representation can miss the intervention’s effects. In a reproduction, record the exact
tensor used by the probe, its normalization, and its dimensionality.
Before moving on. Draw the pipeline from augmentation to encoder, projector,
nonnegative transformation and classifier. Indicate which parameters move during a
linear probe.

4.1
Sparse autoencoder mathematics
A sparse autoencoder takes a pretrained representation r and constructs sparse coefficients a:
a = TopK(Wer + be),
ˆr = Wda + bd.
(4.1)
TopK retains a prescribed number of entries according to the implementation’s activation con-
vention. In a nonnegative SAE, it is applied to nonnegative activations. The reconstruction loss
is
LSAE = E ∥r −ˆr∥2
2 .
(4.2)
Some formulations instead penalize an ℓ1 norm; the paper uses a TopK-style constraint. The
exact bias convention differs between the displayed versions. Do not substitute one formulation
for another silently when reproducing results.
The columns of Wd are dictionary directions. Their coefficients say which directions are active for
each input. A sparse dictionary can reveal features that are mixed in the original neuron basis.
But a successful reconstruction is not sufficient to establish faithful recovery of the network’s
causal computation.
4.2
Why reconstruction and interpretability compete
Reducing the number of active coefficients limits how much information can pass through the
autoencoder. It may encourage selectivity, but it can discard fine detail. Increasing dictionary
width adds representational capacity, but feature splitting or duplicated directions can make
semantic accounting difficult.
A useful evaluation reports at least reconstruction quality, activity/coverage, semantic selectivity
and downstream behavior. A feature that fires once on one correctly labeled image has perfect
apparent label purity but poor coverage.
A dictionary can also make a representation look
cleaner by omitting information.

4.4
LoRA and the nonlinear variant
A conventional low-rank adaptation updates a frozen linear transformation by
y = W0x + ABx,
B ∈Rr×din,
A ∈Rdout×r.
(4.4)
Its parameter cost is r(din + dout). Because AB is a fixed matrix, it can ordinarily be merged
into W0 for inference.
The paper’s MonoLoRA inserts ReLUs into the adaptation branch:
y = W0x + ReLU(A ReLU(B ReLU(x))) .

safety better under some fine-tuning conditions is empirical. A theoretical attribution solely to
semantic purity would require further controls.

The paper also inspects the classifiers trained with 90% noisy labels. For each class it selects the

5.8
The larger toy experiment before the theorem
The paper’s empirical toy uses n = 40, m = 20 and inactive probability S = 0.2. Each nonzero
feature is uniform on [0, 1], and the classification label is the index of the largest feature. The
paper writes the evaluated linear representation as W ⊤Wx, which has n output coordinates
but rank at most m; the compressed code Wx itself has m coordinates. Keeping these distinct
prevents a dimension-counting confusion.
The non-superposed Gram matrix is diagonal with only m nonzero entries, so it stores only
half the concepts. The superposed representation stores more. A linear classifier is trained on
frozen features. The label-noise condition corrupts training labels by choosing another class;
the input-noise condition trains on clean examples and corrupts validation inputs. The plotted
accuracies are approximately 0.50 versus 0.58 on clean data, 0.31 versus 0.26 with label noise,
and 0.19 versus 0.16 with input noise, for mono versus poly respectively.
This is an actual accuracy tradeoff in a larger toy experiment. It should be distinguished from
the two-feature theorem’s J score. The empirical observation motivates the theory but does not
make that score an exact surrogate for accuracy.

11.1
Lab 1: verify the distribution
Generate N samples by multiplying independent uniform amplitudes by Bernoulli activation
masks. Use the strict comparison Y = (X1 > X2), so the zero-zero tie enters class 0. Estimate
class probabilities, means and variances separately.
x = (rng.random((N, 2)) >= S) * rng.random((N, 2))
y = (x[:, 0] > x[:, 1]).astype(int)
mono = x[:, 0]
poly = x[:, 0] - x[:, 1]
for c in (0, 1):
mean = poly[y == c].mean()
variance = poly[y == c].var()
As N increases, the estimates should approach Lecture 7’s formulas. An error concentrated in
class 0 often signals a mishandled atom or tie. An error in both variances often signals forgetting
to subtract the squared mean.
11.2
Lab 2: verify corruption laws
For label noise, generate an independent Bernoulli flip mask and regroup the unchanged rep-
resentation values by eY . Compare empirical moments with the mixture formulas. For input
noise, perturb both coordinates independently but retain the original clean labels; compare the
variance increments with λ2 and 2λ2.
Do not regenerate labels from noisy inputs for this check. That would change the target and
answer a different question. This kind of protocol mismatch can produce a plausible-looking
plot while invalidating the intended derivation.
11.3
Lab 3: score versus actual error
Train a scalar threshold on one sample and evaluate on a separate sample. Plot error and J
in different panels. The independent calculations accompanying this course already perform a
version of this check. The purpose is to see whether the operational quantity of interest follows
the illustrative score, rather than assuming that it does.

11.5
Worked problem A: an atom changes the prior
Question. Why is the class-0 probability not 1/2? Solution. Exchange symmetry makes the
strict events X1 > X2 and X2 > X1 equally probable. Their probabilities sum to 1 −S2, since
only the zero-zero tie has positive tie probability. Each strict event therefore has probability
(1 −S2)/2. Assigning the tie to class 0 adds S2, giving (1 + S2)/2.
11.6
Worked problem B: random noise along a readout
Question. For z = a⊤X and independent zero-mean input noise with covariance N, what is
the variance increment? Solution. The increment is a⊤Na. For isotropic noise N = λ2I,
it is λ2 ∥a∥2
2. This explains the paper’s factors one and two, and immediately generalizes to
correlated noise. It says nothing by itself about a worst-case attack radius.
11.7
Worked problem C: equal clean performance is a control
Question. Why compare robustness at matched clean accuracy as well as at matched architec-
ture? Solution. A representation that drops many difficult features can appear stable simply
because it solves a smaller effective problem. Matched architecture controls resources; matched
clean accuracy helps control how much useful information was retained. Neither control is com-
plete alone. Report the clean-versus-corrupt performance curve, not just the best noisy point.
11.8
Worked problem D: semantic purity without coverage
Question. A neuron fires on only one example, which belongs to class 7. Is its purity score
evidence of a strong class-7 feature? Solution. Its empirical purity is one, but coverage is
negligible and uncertainty is enormous. A useful feature should generalize across examples and
withstand controlled interventions. Report activation count and held-out behavior alongside
purity.
11.9
Worked problem E: complete versus partial representation
Question. Can G = W ⊤W equal In if W has m < n rows? Solution. No. rank(G) ≤m <
n = rank(In). A fixed-capacity monosemantic comparator must discard features or use another
resource allocation. This is central to a meaningful superposition tradeoff.

Chapter 12
Synthesis and the bridge to your research
Learning objective. State the paper’s contribution accurately and carry its moti-
vation into the agreed two-project program.
12.1
The paper in one connected explanation
The paper investigates whether more semantically coherent representations can improve prac-
tical robustness rather than merely making models easier to inspect. It encourages such rep-
resentations through nonnegative contrastive learning and sparse autoencoders, and measures
downstream behavior under noisy labels, corrupted images, distribution shifts and limited super-
vision. A separate nonlinear adapter intervention tests safety retention during language-model
fine-tuning. The reported patterns support a useful connection between representation organi-
zation and reliable behavior.
The toy model explains one possible tradeoff.
A one-dimensional representation containing
both features can separate clean classes better than one that drops a feature. Under certain
corruptions, its conditional moments change in a way that worsens a chosen separability score
relative to the simpler representation. The mathematical statement is deliberately narrower
than a general robustness theorem. The conference additions strengthen the empirical case and
provide a latent-class explanation for why nonnegative representations can arise.
12.2
The first project remains exactly the first project
The superposition-to-robustness phase diagram asks for a boundary or bound as feature
frequency, capacity load and importance vary. To go beyond the source paper, define an oper-
ational risk, include the information lost by a monosemantic baseline, and derive the tradeoff
under stated assumptions. Nonlinear decoding must be retained where it is responsible for sparse
superposition’s benefit.
The source paper is the conceptual and experimental anchor. It should be cited as motivating
evidence, not described as a special case of a new theorem until the assumptions and metrics
genuinely contain its setting.
12.3
The second project remains exactly the second project
Model collapse as monosemanticity decay asks whether feature organization predicts or
contributes to degradation across recursive training. The phrase is a research hypothesis, not an
established result: some loops preserve a distribution, some lose rare combinations, some blur
or amplify features, and a representation can change without a meaningful loss of quality.
The extension should define a training-and-generation operator, measure feature-specific errors
and derive how these errors propagate. A fixed Gram-matrix covariance identity is a useful

elementary calculation, but it does not by itself describe a retrained generative model.
12.4
The combined paper
The shared object is feature interference geometry. Its immediate effect is decoding reliability;
its repeated effect, under an explicitly defined generation mechanism, can be distributional drift.
Volume II develops this connection with exact toy formulas, a transition operator and a coupling
bound. It then explains what needs to be checked in a diffusion model.
The structure remains mathematics, controlled toy validation, real-model evidence.
The derivations are not symbolic-regression fits. The real-model work is scientifically necessary
for the intended empirical story, even though its exact outcome cannot be promised before
experiments.

Questions you should now be able to answer
1. What intervention produces the claimed semantic change, and where in the network is it

3. Why can sparse features share dimensions, and why does the decoder matter?
4. What does the paper’s J measure, and why is it not an adversarial radius or classification-

5. Which empirical observations motivate a broader phase diagram?
6. What additional operator and assumptions are needed to study recursive self-training?
